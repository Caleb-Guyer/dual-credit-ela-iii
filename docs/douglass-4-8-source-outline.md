# Frederick Douglass, Chapters IV–VIII: source and adaptation

Canonical text: _Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave_ (1845), [Project Gutenberg primary transcript](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/23/23-h/23-h.htm#link2HCH0004). No additional classroom excerpt was supplied for this unit. All five chapters were read in full before authoring their scenes and questions. The complete text is bundled in `src/douglass-next/source.json`: 45 paragraphs, including the embedded Whittier verse. References such as `7.4` mean chapter and paragraph in that bundled transcript, not printed page numbers.

## IV — institutional violence and the silencing of witnesses

- 4.1: Hopkins's short tenure; Douglass does not know why it ended. Austin Gore moves from an out-farm to the home plantation.
- 4.2–3: Gore's ambition, cruelty, severity and deference to owners. Enslaved people are denied a defense; accusation, conviction and punishment follow each other.
- 4.4: Demby enters the creek to escape whipping. After three calls, Gore kills him.
- 4.5: Gore argues that disobedience would spread. Lloyd accepts his defense; Gore remains overseer. Enslaved witnesses cannot bring suit or testify; there is no judicial investigation.
- 4.6–7: Thomas Lanman's boast of killings; Mrs. Hicks kills a young girl after the exhausted girl fails to wake for a crying baby. A warrant is issued but not served.
- 4.8–10: Beal Bondly kills an elderly man who crosses a property boundary while gathering food. Douglass explicitly does not know why Bondly visits Lloyd afterward. The event is hushed up.
- **Playable treatment:** first-person exploration of a stylized path and creek, four testimony stops, a causal connection, and a five-question challenge. No playable killing, invented rescue, or violent reward.

## V — deprivation, the voyage and hope

- 5.1: Childhood chores, errands for Lucretia, help finding Daniel Lloyd's shot birds; Daniel's protection and sharing cakes.
- 5.2–3: Hunger, especially cold, inadequate clothing, a corn bag for shelter, communal mush and insufficient food.
- 5.4–5: News of the move to Hugh Auld's household, three days of washing, Lucretia's promised trousers.
- 5.6: Family separation has damaged the ties of home. Cousin Tom's descriptions help create hope for Baltimore.
- 5.7–9: Saturday departure from Miles River; last look aft, then looking ahead from the bow; brief Annapolis stop without going ashore; Sunday arrival at Smith's Wharf. He helps drive the sheep to Curtis's slaughterhouse. Rich conducts him to the Aulds' home.
- 5.10: Hugh, Sophia and their little son Thomas. Sophia's initial kindness. Douglass is to care for Thomas.
- 5.11–12: Retrospective significance of the move and his understanding of providence and enduring hope.
- **Playable treatment:** walk the sloop, follow the transition to the wharf, guide three sheep into a delivery area, and reach the doorway. Three sheep, their following behavior, the pen and dock layout are game abstractions; the source says a large flock and names the slaughterhouse. Douglass does not steer the sloop in the game.

## VI — literacy reveals how slavery works

- 6.1: Sophia previously worked as a weaver, has not controlled an enslaved person before, and initially treats Douglass kindly.
- 6.2: Douglass describes the corrupting effects of irresponsible power on her character.
- 6.3: Alphabet and short-word lessons; Hugh forbids further teaching. His opposition reveals to Douglass that ignorance supports enslavement and knowledge can challenge it. Douglass resolves to learn despite the loss of his teacher.
- 6.4: Generally better city conditions and the restraining effect of neighbors' opinions, qualified by the brutal treatment and hunger of Henrietta and Mary in the Hamilton household.
- **Playable treatment:** explore the home and street, meet Sophia and Hugh one at a time, practice an illustrative letter exercise and identify the unintended lesson of Hugh's objection. The exercise is labeled an illustration, not a documented classroom exchange. Hugh's brief quotation is checked against 6.3.

## VII — self-education and its emotional cost

- 7.1–3: About seven years in Hugh's family; no regular teacher. Sophia moves from kindness to active opposition, seizing a newspaper and watching Douglass's activity.
- 7.4: Street friendships, hurrying errands to make room for lessons, bread exchanged for knowledge. Names are withheld to protect the boys. Conversations about their future freedom and his enslavement for life.
- 7.5–6: _The Columbian Orator_, its master–slave dialogue and Sheridan's Catholic-emancipation speech. Reading gives language to his ideas and arguments, while deepening anguish over oppression without a present remedy.
- 7.7: Hope restrains despair. A newspaper's report on northern petitions explains abolition more clearly than the dictionary. Two Irishmen at Waters's wharf advise escape. He fears a trap, remembers their advice, resolves to wait and first learn writing.
- 7.8: Durgin and Bailey's timber markings: L/S, F/A combinations. Copying letters, friendly challenges to boys, chalk on fences, walls and pavement, Webster's spelling book, and spaces in Thomas's copybooks during Sophia's Monday meetings. Learning takes years.
- **Playable treatment:** carry bread through a city route, exchange it for a lesson, hear the reported dockside question and decode timber markings. The boy's reported writing challenge is presented as a remembered exchange; no name is invented. Blocks and streets are illustrative, not a historical survey.

## VIII — being treated as property, family separation and renewed resolve

- 8.1: Richard dies, then Anthony dies without a will. Andrew and Lucretia divide the estate. Douglass is recalled on Captain Rowe's _Wild Cat_ for valuation. The chapter looks back within the years described previously; it is not simply the next year after Chapter VII.
- 8.2–4: People ranked with livestock; no voice over family separation. Fear of Andrew and his assault on Douglass's brother.
- 8.5: Assigned to Lucretia, Douglass returns to Hugh's family after about a month away. Relief is not emancipation.
- 8.6–8: Lucretia and Andrew die; property passes to strangers; no enslaved person is freed. Douglass condemns the abandonment of his grandmother after lifelong service. Her loneliness is presented through reflection and the Whittier verse. He does not assert that he witnessed her death; the conditional wording is preserved in the bundled source.
- 8.9–11: Thomas's second marriage to Rowena Hamilton, quarrel with Hugh, move to St. Michael's and painful separation from the boys. On the _Amanda_, Douglass studies Philadelphia-bound steamboats turning northeast at North Point and renews his resolve to escape when a chance comes.
- **Playable treatment:** explore estate and woodland spaces, reconstruct the valuation/return sequence, consider the grandmother passage, and observe the direction north. The cabin is explicitly a remembered image, not an invented visit or deathbed scene. The campaign ends while Douglass remains enslaved.

## Presentation and assessment

- Five independently selectable games; four story moments per chapter; 42 short voiced lines.
- Thirty distinct multiple-choice questions, six per chapter. Each chapter completion draws five, shuffles answer positions, explains results, and offers retry of missed answers.
- Full story, activities, question prompts and explanations have bundled speech: 124 locally generated MP3 clips. Voices are synthetic, not archival recordings, human-actor performances or clones of historical people. Reported quotations are labeled; all other factual narration is paraphrased.
- One visible guide at a time. Terrain, architecture, travel compression, jumping, compass, objective markers, music and movement challenges are original illustrative devices.
- Source references remain accessible from dialogue and feedback. Browser saves for this unit are independent of Franklin and Douglass Chapters I–III.
