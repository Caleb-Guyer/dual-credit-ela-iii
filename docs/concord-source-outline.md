# One Night in Concord: source and adaptation outline

The campaign studies Henry David Thoreau's 1849 essay, originally published as **Resistance to Civil Government**, commonly titled **Civil Disobedience**. The source is the full public-domain essay transcribed in [Project Gutenberg ebook 71](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/71/71-h/71-h.htm), retrieved October 24, 2026. Its account of a night in prison is the narrative anchor; the game is an interpretation and teaching adaptation, not a reconstruction of the exact historical geography or a transcript of historical conversations.

## The bundled text

`src/concord/source.json` contains all **51 substantive paragraph and verse blocks**, approximately **9,337 words**, from the opening motto through the final imagined state. The four verse blocks remain in order and are numbered alongside prose. HTML markup and layout whitespace are removed; words, punctuation, historical phrasing, and the author's inserted note about Webster are retained. Some typographical readings come from the source transcription, including “did nor” and “Out love”; these are not silently represented as modern corrections.

**Every paragraph number in the game is an internal reference to this bundled transcription. It is not a page number, a line number, or a promise of matching the numbering in a teacher's edition.** Full-essay coverage is the default because no classroom excerpt or study guide was supplied. A class may assign a shorter excerpt or emphasize different details.

## Four-act adaptation

1. **The common / conscience:** Three discoverable evidence cards (`c01`–`c03`) establish conscience, the limits of majority rule, and the difference between a person and a mechanical instrument. Elias, a neighbor on the common, gives a fictional voice to conformity.
2. **The tax encounter / participation:** Cards `c04`–`c06` examine the minimum duty to withdraw support, counter friction, and the majority of one. The collector's dialogue is an original adaptation of the essay's questions, not a claim to a documented conversation.
3. **The jail / freedom:** Cards `c07`–`c09` distinguish moral integrity, freedom of thought, and coercive strength. Jonas, the fictional jailer beyond the cell bars, presents the state’s arguments; he is not a historical official quoted from records.
4. **Dawn / what comes next:** Cards `c10`–`c12` examine the practical demand for better government, neighborly duty, consent, and the unfinished work of respecting the individual. The morning reader is fictional.

Scenery, object placement, routes, light, physical encounters, debate ordering, and dramatic transitions are original illustrations. The game rearranges the essay's ideas into an evening-to-morning arc. It does not claim the essay was written in a single night, that its full argument was spoken in jail, or that a particular tax dollar was traced to a specific expenditure. Thoreau explicitly declines that latter claim in paragraph 40.

The twelve physical encounters use short button presses, holds, and conversations: a noticeboard, a neighbor on the common, a printing press, the collector’s bill, a merchant’s ledger, the jail door, a quill and paper, a barred window, the cell bed, a morning newspaper, the river bench, and a roadside letterbox. They introduce each source card; completing a gesture does not count as a correct quiz answer. Scene dialogue is labeled as an original adaptation. The collector’s coin can be offered as a reversible preview, but the scene continues through refusal rather than inventing a payment by Thoreau. The ledger explicitly distinguishes withdrawing allegiance from tracing the destination of a particular tax dollar. The cell offers reflection and observation; its door is not an escape challenge.

## Debate and assessment design

- **12 evidence cards** quote exact text from the bundled source. Each card has a distinct interpretive purpose and a paragraph reference.
- **Four debates**, each with **three rounds**, require both a defensible claim and evidence that directly supports it. A correct claim is scored for accurately representing the text, not for matching a player's personal political views. Wrong claims receive specific explanations rather than generic failure messages.
- Debates begin by approaching their characters in the 3D scene. Character-anchored subtitles, gestures, replies, and a compact quotation notebook keep the world visible. Player replies are short conversational paraphrases mapped to the same canonical claims and scoring. Rejoinders and farewells are original adaptation dialogue; the supporting quotations remain exact source text. Stepping away preserves the current exchange, and reading explanations remain available without replacing the conversation with a quiz screen.
- **72 reading-check questions** cover eight categories, with nine questions in each: government and conscience; majority and voting; complicity and action; taxes and resistance; prison and perspective; close-reading details; rhetoric and vocabulary; reform and the individual.
- Each question has four options, one correct answer, a substantive explanation, and an explanation aligned with every individual option. Correct answer positions rotate across the bank. In-game selection may also shuffle questions or answers without changing their meaning.
- Questions preserve uncertainty where the text does: the cellmate denies burning the barn and Thoreau conjectures; the unnamed person who paid the poll tax remains unnamed in questions about the essay; one night of confinement is distinguished from six years of refusal and the cellmate's months awaiting trial.

The practice system supports learning and self-checking. Completing it cannot guarantee an unknown teacher's quiz score. Classroom materials can be used later to narrow or extend the pool without replacing the source-backed explanations.

## Interpretive safeguards

Do not teach that every law should be broken, every tax should be refused, every minority is right, every prisoner is innocent, democracy has no value, government must immediately disappear, or personal desire is interchangeable with conscience. Thoreau's argument includes conditions, practical distinctions, ongoing self-examination, and a hoped-for better state. His polemical claims should be identified as his claims rather than treated as universally established political facts.

The original text contains historically dated language and comparisons. It is retained in the source reader for fidelity; the game's original narration and assessment language use contemporary respectful wording.
